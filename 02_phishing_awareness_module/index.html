📚 What is Phishing?

Phishing is a cyber attack where attackers impersonate legitimate organizations or individuals to steal sensitive information like passwords, credit card numbers, or personal data.

Types of Phishing

📧

Email Phishing

Mass emails sent to thousands, hoping some will click. Often generic greetings like "Dear Customer".

🎯

Spear Phishing

Targeted attacks on specific individuals/organizations. Uses personal info to appear legitimate.

🐋

Whaling

Targets high-profile executives (CEOs, CFOs). Highly customized with extensive research.

📱

Smishing/Vishing

SMS (smishing) or voice call (vishing) phishing. Creates urgency via phone/text.

🔗

Clone Phishing

Copies a legitimate email but replaces links/attachments with malicious versions.

💼

Business Email Compromise

Impersonates executives to request wire transfers or sensitive data from employees.

🎯 Why This Training Matters

Phishing is the #1 attack vector for cybercriminals because it exploits human psychology rather than technical vulnerabilities. No firewall or antivirus can fully protect against a well-crafted phishing email that tricks you into voluntarily giving away credentials.

  • ✅ You are the last line of defense - Technology filters catch ~90%, but 10% reach your inbox
  • ✅ One click can compromise everything - Your credentials, company data, financial systems
  • ✅ Attacks are getting sophisticated - AI-generated content, perfect replicas, personalized context
  • ✅ Reporting stops attacks - Reporting helps IT block senders and warn others

🚩 Red Flags in Phishing Emails

Learn to spot these warning signs:

⚠️
Urgent/Threatening Language

"Immediate action required!", "Account will be closed in 24 hours!", "Urgent: Security Alert"

Attackers create panic to bypass critical thinking. Legitimate companies rarely threaten immediate closure via email.
📧
Generic Greetings

"Dear Customer", "Hello User", "Hi Team", "Valued Member"

Legitimate companies usually address you by name or reference your account number.
🔗
Mismatched URLs

Link text says "paypal.com" but hovering reveals "paypal-security.xyz/login"

Always hover before clicking! Check the actual destination in the bottom-left of your browser.
📝
Poor Grammar, Spelling, Formatting

"We detect unussual activity on you're account. Please verify you're identity."

While sophisticated attacks can be well-crafted, many phishing emails have telltale errors.
📎
Unexpected Attachments

Invoices, receipts, shipping notifications, "scanned documents" you weren't expecting

Malware often hides in .exe, .zip, .scr, .pdf, .docx files. Verify with sender via another channel.
🎁
Too Good to Be True

"You won a $500 gift card!", "Refund of $1,200 approved", "Free iPhone 15"

If you didn't enter a contest, you didn't win. Legitimate refunds don't require login via email links.
🔐
Requests for Credentials/Payments

"Verify your password", "Update payment info", "Confirm SSN for security"

Legitimate companies never ask for passwords, PINs, or full SSN via email.
✅
Green Flags (Likely Legitimate)

Addresses you by name, references recent transaction/order #, no links asking for login, directs you to log in directly via app/website (not email link)

🌐 Spotting Fake Websites

Phishing sites mimic legitimate ones. Check these elements:

🔒

Check the URL Carefully

  • HTTPS ≠ Safe (phishers use SSL too)
  • Look for: paypal.com vs paypal.com.phishersite.com
  • Subdomain trick: security.paypal.com.attacker.com
  • Typosquatting: paypa1.com, amaz0n.com, micros0ft.com
🔍

Inspect Site Details

  • Padlock icon ≠ legitimate (only means encrypted)
  • Check certificate: Click padlock → "Certificate is valid"
  • Look for "Not Secure" warnings
  • Poor design, broken links, low-res images
📱

Behavioral Red Flags

  • Immediate login pop-up
  • Requests 2FA codes via page
  • Asks for info they should already have
  • Redirects to real site after stealing data
🛡️

Verification Steps

  • Type URL manually, don't click email links
  • Use bookmarks for sensitive sites
  • Check with password manager (won't autofill on fake domains)
  • Use browser phishing protection

🧠 Psychology of Social Engineering

Phishing exploits human psychology. Understanding these tactics makes you immune to manipulation.

⏰

Urgency & Fear

"Your account will be deleted in 1 hour!"

Counter: Pause. Breathe. Verify independently. No legitimate emergency requires instant email action.

👮

Authority Impersonation

"This is IT Security / CEO / IRS / Police"

Counter: Verify through official channels. Real authorities don't demand credentials via email.

🤝

Trust & Familiarity

"Hi John, per our conversation..." (references fake meetings)

Counter: Check your calendar. Contact the person directly. Don't trust context alone.

🎁

Reciprocity & Rewards

"Free gift for completing survey!" "Bonus for early response!"

Counter: If it sounds too good to be true, it is. Legitimate rewards don't require credentials.

😰

Fear of Missing Out (FOMO)

"Limited time offer!" "Only 3 spots left!" "Expires today!"

Counter: Artificial scarcity. Take time to verify. Real opportunities don't vanish in minutes.

🎭

Social Proof

"10,000 employees already completed this!" "Your colleagues have verified"

Counter: Bandwagon effect. Verify independently. Popularity ≠ legitimacy.

🎯 Advanced Tactics to Watch

Multi-Channel Attacks

Attackers combine email + phone + SMS. Example: Email says "IT will call you," then caller asks for 2FA code.

Conversation Hijacking

Attacker compromises a vendor's email, replies to existing thread with "updated invoice" containing malware.

OAuth Phishing (Consent Phishing)

Fake "Sign in with Microsoft/Google" prompts that request app permissions instead of passwords. Grants persistent access.

QR Code Phishing (Quishing)

Malicious QR codes in emails, flyers, menus. Harder to inspect than URLs. Always preview URL before opening.

AI-Enhanced Attacks

Perfect grammar, personalized context from LinkedIn/social media, deepfake voice/video for vishing.

📋 Real-World Phishing Examples

Example 1: Microsoft 365 Credential Harvest

PHISHING
From: Microsoft Security 
Subject: Unusual sign-in activity on your account

Hi there,

We detected a sign-in from a new device:
📍 Location: Berlin, Germany
💻 Device: Chrome on Windows
🕐 Time: 2 hours ago

If this wasn't you, secure your account immediately:
[Review Recent Activity] → malicious-link.xyz/microsoft/login

If this was you, you can ignore this email.

Microsoft Security Team

Red flags: Sender domain (microsoft-account-team.com ≠ microsoft.com), generic "Hi there", pressure to click link, link goes to .xyz domain.

Example 2: CEO Fraud / Business Email Compromise

PHISHING
From: Sarah Johnson 
To: finance@company.com
Subject: URGENT - Wire Transfer Needed Today

Hi [Finance Manager Name],

I'm in a board meeting and need you to process an urgent 
wire transfer for a confidential acquisition.

Amount: $47,500.00
Beneficiary: Global Tech Solutions Ltd
Account: [Redacted - reply for details]

This is time-sensitive. Please confirm once sent.

Sent from my iPhone

Red flags: CEO domain mismatch, urgency, secrecy ("confidential"), requests bypassing normal approval process, "sent from iPhone" to explain brevity.

Example 3: Legitimate Email (for comparison)

LEGITIMATE
From: Amazon.com 
Subject: Your Amazon.com order #112-3456789-1234567

Hello John Smith,

Thank you for your order. We've received your order #112-3456789-1234567 
for delivery on Tuesday, March 15.

Order Details:
• Wireless Keyboard - $49.99
• Estimated Total: $53.97

Track your package: [Link goes to amazon.com/your-orders]
View order: [Link goes to amazon.com/your-orders]

No action needed. This is a confirmation only.

Amazon.com

Green flags: Real domain (amazon.com), personal name, specific order #, no urgency, links go to known domain, no login request.

🎭 Social Engineering Scenario: "IT Support" Call

VISHING (Voice Phishing)
Attacker: "Hi, this is Mike from IT Support. We're seeing 
         unusual login attempts on your account from 
         another country. For security, I need to verify 
         your identity. Can you read me the 6-digit code 
         that was just sent to your phone?"

Victim: "Sure, it's 847291."

Attacker: "Thanks! Your account is now secure. I'll 
         block those foreign IPs. Have a good day!"

Result: Attacker now has full access to your account 
        (used the 2FA code to log in themselves).

Lesson: Never share 2FA codes with anyone. IT support never needs your 2FA code. If unsure, hang up and call IT directly using the official number.

🛡️ Before You Click - The STOP Method

S

S - Stop

Don't click immediately. Take a breath. Phishing relies on impulse.

T

T - Think

Was I expecting this? Does it create urgency? Is the sender legitimate?

O

O - Observe

Hover links. Check sender address. Look for red flags. Verify independently.

P

P - Proceed/Report

If suspicious: Report it, delete it. If legitimate: Navigate directly via app/bookmark.

✅ Daily Security Habits

Email Safety

  • ✅ Verify sender address - Not just display name (easy to spoof)
  • ✅ Hover all links - Check actual destination before clicking
  • ✅ Don't open unexpected attachments - Verify via phone/Teams first
  • ✅ Use "Report Phishing" button - Helps protect everyone
  • ✅ Check for digital signatures - Some orgs sign emails cryptographically

Credential Protection

  • ✅ Use a password manager - Won't autofill on fake domains
  • ✅ Enable MFA everywhere - Prefer authenticator apps over SMS
  • ✅ Never reuse passwords - Especially not work/personal
  • ✅ Never share passwords/2FA codes - Not even with "IT support"
  • ✅ Use passkeys where available - Phishing-resistant authentication

Device & Network Security

  • ✅ Keep software updated - Browser, OS, apps patch phishing exploits
  • ✅ Use DNS filtering - Blocks known phishing domains (Quad9, NextDNS)
  • ✅ Don't bypass browser warnings - "Proceed anyway" is dangerous
  • ✅ Separate work/personal browsing - Different profiles/browsers
  • ✅ Lock screen when away - Prevents physical access attacks

🚨 If You Clicked / Fell Victim

Don't panic. Act quickly:

1️⃣

Immediate: Change Passwords

Change the compromised account + any accounts using same password. Use password manager to generate unique ones.

2️⃣

Enable/Check MFA

Turn on authenticator app MFA. Review authorized devices/sessions and revoke unknown ones.

3️⃣

Report Immediately

IT/Security team, bank (if financial), email provider's abuse address, FTC (reportfraud.ftc.gov).

4️⃣

Scan for Malware

Run full antivirus scan. Check for suspicious browser extensions. Monitor accounts for unusual activity.

5️⃣

Freeze Credit (if PII stolen)

Contact Equifax, Experian, TransUnion. Free credit freeze prevents new accounts in your name.

6️⃣

Learn & Share

Review what happened. Share with colleagues (anonymized). You're not alone - it happens to experts too.
